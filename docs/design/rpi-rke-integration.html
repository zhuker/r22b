Raspberry Pi ↔ OEM RKE integration — design details

Drafted 2026-09-13. Companion doc to dash22b-rpi-gateway.html (overall Pi gateway plan, Phase 1 = BLE door lock) and oem-rke-retrofit.html (how the 2001 body harness is provisioned for factory keyless entry). This doc is the technical bridge between the two: how the Pi actually drives the door-lock circuit, given that the '01 body already has a factory-terminated RKE connector under the CEL/DLC bundle waiting to be used.

Updated 2026-09-13 after reading the FSM and checking the car. The first draft of this doc assumed an ACC pin, a keyless LED pin and an interior-lamp trigger pin on the connector. The FSM shows none of those. Everything below uses the verified pinout; the full derivation and schematic crops are in oem-rke-retrofit.html section 3.

The connector: B176 and its dummy B241

On this car there is a 10-pin connector taped next to the OBD-II connector. The FSM calls it B176; on non-keyless cars it gets factory dummy plug B241, drawn on WI-34 as one red/blue (RL) jumper across pins 6 and 10. This car has a hand-made plain wire jumper instead of the factory plug, on the same two pins: blue-white (LW) and red-green (RG).

Measured so far: pin 9 (YR) is the unlock request and is active-low: 12 V at rest, pulled to ground while the door lock switch is held at unlock. Proven on the car: a direct ~0.5 s jumper from YR to ground unlocked the doors; a 2.2 kΩ jumper did not, so the output must be a low-resistance switch to ground (a MOSFET or SSR in the ohms, not a weak pull). Pin 7 (LG) measured the same way for lock, and a ~0.5 s jumper from LG to ground locked the doors. Both Pi actions are proven by hand on the car. Pin 10 has no static lock state, but pulses to 11.7 V on unlock (1.5 V on lock). Still to confirm: LgB mirroring RG on lock, the minimum pulse length the timer accepts.

Three integration topologies

Topology A — Layered: OEM receiver + Pi tap in parallel (recommended)

Install the OEM keyless retrofit first (~$50–$80 used H7110AS011 module + GOH-M24 fobs off eBay, plug-in install, reversible). The Pi comes later and taps the same lock/unlock lines the OEM module drives (B176 pins 7 and 9), running in parallel to the OEM circuit rather than replacing it.

Why this is the default choice

  • Immediate ergonomic win. OEM retrofit ships this weekend for lunch money and closes the "no keyless" gap while the Pi work spans months.
  • Independent fallback. Pi crashed, BLE stack wedged after a firmware update, phone battery dead → the OEM fob still works because it runs on a completely independent Subaru circuit that the Pi can't take down.
  • Pi acquires a natural staging point. Bench-testing the Pi's relay board is trivial: hook a scope to the OEM receiver's LOCK/UNLOCK outputs, capture what a real fob press looks like, replicate exactly on the Pi.
  • Two independent auth paths. The car has two things that can unlock it: an RF fob (proximity-limited, no crypto, but simple) and a BLE-paired phone (crypto-authenticated, walkaway-capable). Different threat models, different failure modes — good defense in depth.

Physical arrangement

  • OEM H7110AS011 module on its factory stud behind the fuse block, connected to the factory harness the OEM way.
  • Pi hardware in the glovebox with the rest of the Pi (see gateway doc). Pi's relay board has two output lines, wired to the same LOCK / UNLOCK command wires between the OEM receiver and the door-lock timer.
  • Two mounting options for the parallel tap:
    • Y-harness (preferred). Fabricate a small pigtail: a B176-mating shell on one end, a module-side shell on the other, all 10 pins passed straight through (so pins 6 and 10 keep going through the OEM module as designed). Break out pins 7, 9, 2, 8 to a Molex or Deutsch connector going to the Pi. Fully reversible — pull the harness and the car is stock.
    • T-tap pins 7 and 9 near B176. Simpler but not as clean.

Contention model

Three things share timer inputs B92-1 and B92-5: the door lock switches, the OEM module (pins 9 and 7) and the Pi. The unlock input measured active-low with 12 V at rest, so every source just pulls the line to ground. The lock input measured the same. Several sources pulling low at once should therefore be harmless: the timer sees one longer request and runs one cycle.

Topology B — Pi-only: Pi is the receiver, no OEM fob

Fabricate a B176-mating shell, wire it to the Pi's output board. Unplug dummy B241, plug in the Pi shell. No OEM module purchased.

The Pi shell must bridge pins 6 and 10, exactly like the factory dummy (and the hand-made jumper now on the car). What breaks without it isn't documented, so treat the bridge as mandatory.

Pros

  • Nothing to buy on the OEM side.
  • Tidier install — one custom harness, no OEM module on its factory stud.
  • Slightly better BOM cost.

Cons

  • No independent fallback. Phone dead / Pi crashed → you're back to unlocking with the physical key. Not the end of the world (mechanical key always works), but keyless is unavailable.
  • All eggs in the Pi basket. Any Pi kernel panic, SD card corruption, BLE stack regression, or watchdog misconfiguration takes out keyless entry. With Topology A the OEM fob keeps working through all of those.
  • No OEM fob behavior to copy. The driver's door lock switch drives the same timer inputs, so you can still characterize lock/unlock requests without a module. What you lose is the fob-specific behavior (e.g. the single-press driver-only unlock), which lives in the module, not the timer.

When it makes sense

Only if OEM part sourcing dries up entirely (H7110AS011 is discontinued at Subaru; used market is currently healthy but that changes over time) or if the reversibility/reference-scope arguments don't apply to how you plan to develop.

Topology C — Pi upstream: Pi mimics fob RF (do not build)

Pi transmits a rolling-code RF signal on the OEM receiver's operating frequency (315 or 433 MHz depending on region). OEM receiver decodes it as if a normal fob had been pressed.

Skip this. Three reasons:

  • RF Subaru keyless is relay-attackable — well-documented weakness. Adding another Pi-side transmitter to the mix doesn't improve the security bar; you get exactly the phishing-target properties of the existing OEM fob without any of the crypto benefits of BLE peer auth.
  • Re-implementing rolling-code correctly is a project unto itself. Every fob rollover, every desync, every replay-guard is your problem to reverse-engineer from a live receiver.
  • You've already built a better auth channel in BLE (Ed25519 challenge-response, biometric-gated, hardware-backed key in Android Keystore). Route the phone through that, not through a worse imitation of the OEM fob.

Signaling — what the Pi actually emits

Whichever topology you build, the Pi's job on the door-lock side is to briefly activate B176 pin 7 (LG) or pin 9 (YR), which land on door lock timer 88201FC030 inputs B92-5 and B92-1. Those are the same inputs the door lock switches use, so a Pi request should look to the timer like someone operating the lock switch. The timer then drives the actuators. The Pi never touches actuator wiring.

What to measure before writing firmware

  • Mapping: which of pins 7/9 changes when the driver's door lock switch goes to LOCK, and which on UNLOCK.
  • Active level: idle voltage and active voltage on each (expected: ~12 V or ~5 V idle, pulled to ground when active — unconfirmed).
  • Minimum pulse: the shortest request the timer reliably acts on. Start with ~200 ms and shorten until it stops working, then double the last working value.
  • Lock state: no static signal exists on B176. Three ways to track it, cheapest first:
    1. Remember commands and passively watch pins 7 and 9 (isolated, high-impedance) for door lock button presses.
    2. Watch the motor drive pulses. Pin 10 (RG) measured ~12 V during every unlock and 1.5 V on lock. The other motor wire, LgB (timer B92-3, not on B176), should do the reverse on lock; not yet measured. Reading both through optocouplers catches every powered lock and unlock (button or Pi).
    3. Door sensors (reed switch or microswitch on each door's lock rod) for true position. This is the only option that sees the mechanical key and hand-moved knobs: measured on the car, the key cylinder locks/unlocks only its own door and produces no pulse on RG. Since the key can leave the two doors in different states, full "car locked" status needs a sensor in both doors.

The fob behaviors the forum describes (single-press unlocks the driver's door, hold for all doors) are probably implemented inside the OEM module, not the timer. If the Pi wants the same split, it may need to reproduce the module's request pattern. Scope pins 7/9 during fob presses once an OEM module is installed (Topology A) to see what it actually sends.

Pi-side hardware

Output stage — MOSFETs, not relays

Small logic-level N-channel MOSFETs (e.g. AO3400, or a pre-built breakout board) driven from two Pi GPIO pins. Reasons to prefer MOSFETs over mechanical relays:

  • Silent — no coil-click in the glovebox when the phone approaches.
  • Fast — pulse rise/fall in µs, no relay-actuator latency to worry about.
  • No EMI from coil-crash on release.
  • Lower current draw when idle.

The lock/unlock requests are signal-level inputs to the timer, not actuator power — nothing about them needs a relay's contact rating. Low-side switching is confirmed correct: both lock (LG) and unlock (YR) measured active-low. An opto-isolated solid-state relay is a good alternative, since it also isolates the Pi's ground from the car's.

Hardware watchdog on the enable line

Non-negotiable. A malicious BLE input, a Pi kernel panic, or a buggy service that holds a GPIO high can otherwise leave a lock or unlock request asserted indefinitely. How the timer reacts to a held input isn't documented: it may re-fire, hold the actuators, or ignore the manual switch until the input releases. None of those are acceptable, so the output must be forced off in hardware.

Wire the MOSFET gates through an AND gate with an "enable" line that the Pi must refresh every <200 ms or it goes safe (both gates forced low). A dedicated small MCU (an ATtiny or one of the RP2040 boards) acts as the watchdog, monitoring a GPIO heartbeat from the Pi. If the heartbeat stops, the MCU pulls the enable low and holds it low until the Pi's watchdog service explicitly re-asserts the heartbeat. This gives you three lines of defense against a stuck output:

  1. Pi user-space is misbehaving → the watchdog service on the Pi kills any command that runs longer than 3 s.
  2. Pi kernel is misbehaving → the external MCU stops seeing heartbeats and disables the outputs.
  3. External MCU is misbehaving → a hardware timeout on the enable line itself (an RC + comparator, or a re-triggerable monostable) opens the outputs anyway.

Power

LineSourceUsed for
Pi powerSeparate fused tap (recommended), or B176 pin 3 (W, M/B fuse 7, 20 A)Buck converter to 5 V. See below for why not pin 1.
Ignition ON senseB176 pin 2 (GB, F/B fuse 18)Resistor divider or opto into a GPIO. State-machine input only (car on vs off), never a power source. There is no ACC pin on B176.
GroundB176 pin 8 (B)Common reference for the output stage, so requests share the timer's 0 V.

Why not power the Pi from pin 1. Pin 1 (WB) is on M/B fuse 5, the same fuse that feeds the door lock timer. A fault on the Pi side that pops that fuse would take out the power locks entirely, including the manual switch. Pin 3 (W) is on M/B fuse 7 (20 A), which also feeds the tail & illumination relay, so a fault there costs you tail lights instead. A dedicated fused tap keeps Pi faults away from both. Current is not the issue: a Pi 4 is roughly 200–400 mA at 12 V, well inside either fuse.

Parked power: the Pi gets its own lithium battery (Alex, 2026-09-13), so the Pi's draw while parked doesn't come out of the car's starting battery. Charge that pack only while pin 2 shows ignition ON. That way the car battery is never drained, and pin 3 (or the separate tap) only has to carry charging current.

Fail-safe modes

Enumerate every way the Pi side can break and what happens to the car:

Failure modeSymptomWhat the car does
Pi kernel panicHeartbeat stopsExternal watchdog disables outputs. Car keeps normal factory door-lock behavior (manual switch, OEM fob in Topology A, key cylinder in both).
BLE stack wedgedPhone can't connectNo BLE lock/unlock. OEM fob still works in Topology A. Physical key always works.
Phone dead / offNothing to auth againstSame as above.

Malicious BLE peer replays commandsAttempts to unlockEd25519 challenge-response rejects (rolling nonce, bonded pairing). Rate-limit backoff after 3 fails.
Pi power drops (fuse blown, battery dead)No BLE, no relay driveSame as kernel panic — car reverts to stock behavior. OEM fob (Topology A) works.
Watchdog MCU diesEnable line stuckIf stuck LOW (safe), outputs disabled. If stuck HIGH, the pull-down and the Pi's own watchdog still limit exposure; monitor the MCU's own heartbeat back to the Pi so this is detectable.

Bench-test sequence

Order of operations for actually building this, from safest to riskiest:

  1. Meter B176, no parts needed. Done 2026-09-13: B176 closed by a hand-made jumper on pins 6–10 (LW–RG, same as the factory dummy), pin 3 constant, pin 2 key-ON, pin 9 (YR) unlock and pin 7 (LG) lock, both active-low, and a jumper to ground on each unlocked/locked the car. Pin 10 reads 0 V at rest but pulses ~12 V on unlock, so it works as an unlock-event signal. The mechanical door key produces no pulse and only affects its own door. Next: MIN/MAX on LgB for lock events.
  2. OEM retrofit. Buy the H7110AS011 + fobs, install per the retrofit doc, confirm keyless works. No Pi involvement yet.
  3. Scope pins 7/9 during fob presses. With the module installed, capture what it sends for lock, single unlock and held unlock. That's the pattern to copy if the Pi should behave like the fob.
  4. Bench-build the Pi relay board. Two MOSFETs, one watchdog MCU, one buck converter, no car yet. Drive from a bench Pi with mock GATT commands. Verify the outputs pulse-match what you captured in step 2. Verify the watchdog actually kills outputs on heartbeat loss.
  5. Wire into the car in Topology A. 3-way harness or T-tap between OEM receiver and door-lock timer. Confirm both OEM fob and Pi BLE can independently trigger lock/unlock, and that neither interferes with the other.
  6. Add walkaway auto-lock (Phase 1.5 in the gateway doc) once the manual-command path has been stable through a few weeks of normal use.

What this doesn't cover (yet)

  • Trunk / hatch release. The '01 coupe body doesn't have a factory-actuated trunk release from RKE — the third button on the GOH-M24 fob is a panic/alarm trigger, not a trunk pop. If Alex wants remote trunk from the Pi, that's a separate actuator + wiring project.
  • Door-open state. Pins 5 and 6 are door switch lines per the FSM. Whether a Pi can usefully read door-open state from them needs its own measurement (open each door, watch 5 and 6). The FSM shows no separate keyless LED or interior-lamp trigger pin on B176; an earlier draft of this doc was wrong about that.
  • Alarm / security upgrade. Subaru's H7110FS200 layered an alarm on top of RKE. Out of scope here — the Pi could implement its own soft alarm (door-open detection triggers BLE notification to the phone) without any of the OEM alarm hardware.
